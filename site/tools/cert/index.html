Certificate Decoder

A certificate is DER-encoded ASN.1 — nested type-length-value, all the way down. Paste PEM or drop a DER file: you get the facts (who, until when, which names, what key) and the exact bytes each field lives in. Certificates are public; they're decoded locally and never uploaded.

ASN.1: the format under the format

Everything in a certificate is a TLV — one tag byte (what kind of thing), a length, and the value, which is often more TLVs. 30 opens a SEQUENCE, 02 an INTEGER, 06 an OBJECT IDENTIFIER, A0 a context-specific wrapper. That's why every certificate starts with 30 82 …: a SEQUENCE whose length needs two bytes. The whole X.509 grammar is three fields: the part that gets signed, the algorithm, and the signature. 

Certificate ::= SEQUENCE {
    tbsCertificate       "to be signed": version, serial, issuer,
                         validity, subject, public key, extensions
    signatureAlgorithm   e.g. ecdsa-with-SHA256
    signatureValue       the issuer's signature over tbsCertificate }

OIDs: naming by numbers

Fields aren't named with strings but with object identifiers — dotted numbers from a global registration tree. 2.5.4.3 is "common name", 1.2.840.113549.1.1.11 is "sha256WithRSAEncryption" (840 = USA, 113549 = RSA Data Security). The decoder knows the ones that matter and shows the raw number for the rest.

Three things worth checking on any cert

  • The SAN list, not the CN — browsers only match hostnames against subjectAltName.
  • basicConstraints — a leaf certificate must say CA:FALSE; a CA that can sign anything says CA:TRUE with an optional path length.
  • The validity window — public TLS certificates are capped at 398 days, so automation (ACME) is the norm, not the exception.

Frequently asked questions

Is it safe to paste a certificate here?

Certificates are public by design — they're sent in the clear in every TLS handshake — and this tool decodes them entirely in your browser; nothing is uploaded. Never paste private keys anywhere: the tool refuses PRIVATE KEY PEM blocks outright, because no browser tool should teach you that habit.

Can I paste a whole chain (fullchain.pem)?

Yes. Paste every CERTIFICATE block and the decoder lists the chain — leaf, intermediates, and the root if present — and checks how each link is assembled: the issuer name must equal the next certificate's subject, and the authorityKeyIdentifier must equal its subjectKeyIdentifier. Servers normally omit the root (it lives in the client's trust store), which the panel notes rather than flags. This is assembly checking, not signature verification.

What is the difference between PEM and DER?

DER is the binary encoding of the certificate's ASN.1 structure. PEM is that same DER wrapped in base64 with -----BEGIN CERTIFICATE----- armor so it survives copy-paste and email. Same bytes, two costumes — this tool accepts both, plus bare base64.

Why does my browser ignore the certificate's Common Name?

Hostname matching moved to the subjectAltName extension: RFC 2818 deprecated CN checking in 2000, and Chrome stopped honoring CN entirely in 2017. A certificate without your hostname in its SAN list will fail, no matter what the CN says. The CN survives mostly as a display label.

How do I check when a certificate expires?

Paste it here — the validity line shows both dates and computes the days remaining (or how long ago it expired). The notBefore/notAfter fields use UTCTime for dates through 2049 and GeneralizedTime after, a quirk inherited from RFC 5280.
